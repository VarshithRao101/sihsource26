# Historical validation — the system against seven documented dam failures

Generated by `python -m integration.historical_validation --md`. Every
figure below is computed at generation time; nothing is transcribed by hand.

**Reference:** data/observed/historical_dam_failures.pdf - 'HISTORICAL DAM FAILURES: FIELD PARAMETERS & IMPACT DATA', supplied reference document

## What this measures — and what it does not

**Measured: peak breach outflow.** For each event the published dam
geometry — height, crest length, storage — is fed to the same hydrograph
code the solver uses as its upstream boundary, under the failure mode that
matches the documented mechanism. The result is compared against the peak
discharge the reference document reports.

That comparison is worth making because it is **not circular**. Nothing in
`shared/hydro.py` was fitted to these seven events: Froehlich (2008) is
fitted to 74 embankment failures, the critical-flow coefficient is Ritter's
analytical solution, and the moraine treatment is Huggel et al. (2002).

**Not measured: inundation extent, arrival times, depths, casualties.**
Those require the flood routed over the real terrain, and for five of the
seven that is not a test that can honestly be run:

- Teton and St. Francis no longer have reservoirs; the DEM shows the valley
  as it is now, with the impoundment gone.
- Banqiao was rebuilt in 1993 on the same site with different geometry.
- The Ru River basin below Banqiao has been re-engineered since 1975.
- South Fork's lake was never refilled.

Routing those on a modern DEM and calling the result a hindcast would be
measuring the wrong terrain. Where a routed comparison **is** defensible —
Machchhu II, whose valley is substantially unchanged and which is in the CWC
register — it is run by `integration/machhu1979.py` and reported there.

So this validates the **breach and release physics**. It does not validate
the hydrodynamics.

## Accuracy

```
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  PEAK BREACH OUTFLOW - computed vs reported, seven events                |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                                                          |
|  within a factor of 1.25 .........  1/7   (14% of events)                |
|  within a factor of 2 ............  5/7   (71% of events)                |
|  within a factor of 3 ............  6/7   (86% of events)                |
|  outside a factor of 3 ...........  1/7                                  |
|                                                                          |
|  median absolute error ...........  57.8%                                |
|  mean absolute error .............  107.7%                               |
|  geometric mean ratio ............  1.515   (runs high)                  |
|                                                                          |
|  out-of-sample events ............  4/7   (not in any fitting dataset)   |
|  of those, within factor 2 .......  2/4                                  |
|                                                                          |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**How to read those numbers.** A factor of two on peak breach discharge is a
good result in this field, not a weak one. Wahl (2004), *Uncertainty of
Predictions of Embankment Dam Breach Parameters*, ASCE JHE 130(5), found the
standard breach regressions carry prediction intervals of roughly −0.5 to +1
order of magnitude on peak outflow — that is, a factor of about 3 low to 10
high. Every band above should be read against that, not against an intuition
borrowed from a field where 5% is the target.

The **geometric** mean is used for the ratio rather than the arithmetic mean:
the arithmetic mean of 0.5 and 2.0 is 1.25, which would report a bias where
there is none, because those two errors are the same size in opposite
directions.

## What happened, and what we produced

### 1. Machchhu Dam II — Morbi, Gujarat, India, 11 August 1979

**What actually happened.** Massive overtopping. 600 mm in 24 h produced an inflow of 16,300 m3/s against a design spillway capacity of 5,660 m3/s. Water overtopped the earthen flanks by 0.6 m and scoured away 2,100 m of embankment. The wave travelled about 60 km downstream (6 to 9 m wall, 6-10 m in Morbi city). Reported deaths: 2,000 to 10,000+. Reported peak breach discharge **16,307 m³/s** (stated exactly).

**What our system produced.** Case `overtopping` — The document names overtopping and gives the depth over the flanks. This is the textbook case for the mode. Peak outflow **32,206 m³/s** at 135.0 min, releasing 779.2 MCM in total — the 110.0 MCM stored plus roughly 704.2 MCM that arrived as inflow during the event, which is what the documented rainfall did. Breach parameters from: froehlich2008.

**Agreement.** 1.98× reported (+97.5%), graded *good*. Four independent published peak-outflow regressions put this dam between 8,090 and 29,045 m³/s; the reported figure is inside that envelope.

### 2. Banqiao Dam — Henan Province, China, 8 August 1975

**What actually happened.** Catastrophic overtopping driven by Typhoon Nina, 1,060 mm in 24 h. Sluice gates could not open fully due to siltation blockages, triggering a chain-reaction collapse of 62 dams. The wave travelled about 110 km downstream (6 to 10 m wall at ~50 km/h). Reported deaths: 171,000 to 230,000. Reported peak breach discharge **78,800 m³/s** (stated exactly).

**What our system produced.** Case `spillway_blockage` — The document is explicit that the gates could not open. That is not ordinary overtopping - the reservoir had no relief capacity while the inflow arrived, which is the mode's definition. Peak outflow **181,978 m³/s** at 39.0 min, releasing 1,051.8 MCM in total — the 492.0 MCM stored plus roughly 561.6 MCM that arrived as inflow during the event, which is what the documented rainfall did. Breach parameters from: froehlich2008 (post-overtopping breach).

  This case also produced the quantity no other case can: the reservoir reached the crest **4.93 hours** after the outlets were lost. That is the warning time the operator had.

**Agreement.** 2.31× reported (+130.9%), graded *acceptable*. Four independent published peak-outflow regressions put this dam between 32,545 and 130,112 m³/s; the reported figure is inside that envelope.

> **Input caveat.** Inflow 13,000 m3/s and a design capacity of 1,742 m3/s are the commonly cited figures for Banqiao and are NOT in the reference document. They are inputs we supplied, and the peak below therefore depends on them.

### 3. Teton Dam — Idaho, United States, 5 June 1976

**What actually happened.** Internal erosion and piping. Fractured volcanic rhyolite in the right abutment let reservoir water bypass the grout curtain and wash out the silt core during first filling. The wave travelled about 130 km downstream (2.5-3 m in residential zones; 45 m wide breach). Reported deaths: 11 to 14. Reported peak breach discharge **65,120 m³/s** (stated exactly).

**What our system produced.** Case `piping` — The document names piping and describes the conduit. Peak outflow **102,736 m³/s** at 45.0 min, releasing 356.1 MCM of the 356.0 MCM stored. Breach parameters from: froehlich2008.

**Agreement.** 1.58× reported (+57.8%), graded *good*. Four independent published peak-outflow regressions put this dam between 25,706 and 98,256 m³/s; the reported figure is inside that envelope.

### 4. St. Francis Dam — California, United States, 12 March 1928

**What actually happened.** Geological abutment failure. Gypsum veins in the western abutment dissolved when saturated; the eastern abutment slipped along lubricated Pelona schist fault planes. The wave travelled about 87 km downstream (43 m initial wave in the canyon). Reported deaths: 431 to 450+. Reported peak breach discharge **48,000 m³/s** (~ approximate in the source).

**What our system produced.** Case `foundation_failure` — A concrete gravity dam displaced off its foundation. No embankment eroded, so no embankment regression applies - this is the case the mode was written for. Peak outflow **35,777 m³/s** at 3.0 min, releasing 45.8 MCM of the 47.0 MCM stored. Breach parameters from: none - see FoundationCollapse.basis.

  No breach regression was used. The opening is geometry — 170 m at the crest narrowing to 43 m at the bed — under critical-flow control.

**Agreement.** 0.75× reported (-25.5%), graded *good*. Four independent published peak-outflow regressions put this dam between 9,294 and 40,125 m³/s; the reported figure is **outside** that envelope.

### 5. South Fork Dam (Johnstown) — Pennsylvania, United States, 31 May 1889

**What actually happened.** Crest lowering and debris clogs. The crest had been lowered for a carriage road and iron fish screens across the spillway blocked floating debris, leaving zero capacity in a record storm. The wave travelled about 23 km downstream (12 m wall at 65 km/h). Reported deaths: 2,209. Reported peak breach discharge **8,500 m³/s** (~ approximate in the source).

**What our system produced.** Case `spillway_blockage` — Screens across the spillway and a lowered crest. The relief capacity was gone before the storm arrived, which is the mode. Peak outflow **5,860 m³/s** at 45.0 min, releasing 30.9 MCM in total — the 18.0 MCM stored plus roughly 13.0 MCM that arrived as inflow during the event, which is what the documented rainfall did. Breach parameters from: froehlich2008 (post-overtopping breach).

  This case also produced the quantity no other case can: the reservoir reached the crest **4.33 hours** after the outlets were lost. That is the warning time the operator had.

**Agreement.** 0.69× reported (-31.1%), graded *good*. Four independent published peak-outflow regressions put this dam between 3,873 and 10,746 m³/s; the reported figure is inside that envelope.

> **Input caveat.** The inflow and the spillway capacity are not in the reference document; both are supplied here and the peak depends on them.

### 6. Malpasset Dam — Frejus, France, 2 December 1959

**What actually happened.** Foundation shearing under hydrostatic uplift. First filling exposed unmapped tectonic fault planes in the gneiss beneath the left arch thrust block. The wave travelled about 12 km downstream (40 m wall at 70 km/h). Reported deaths: 421. Reported peak breach discharge **8,000 m³/s** (~ approximate in the source).

**What our system produced.** Case `foundation_failure` — An arch dam that lost its thrust block. Nothing eroded; the arch was found displaced substantially intact. Peak outflow **40,698 m³/s** at 3.0 min, releasing 48.7 MCM of the 50.0 MCM stored. Breach parameters from: none - see FoundationCollapse.basis.

  No breach regression was used. The opening is geometry — 178 m at the crest narrowing to 44 m at the bed — under critical-flow control.

**Agreement.** 5.09× reported (+408.7%), graded *poor*. Four independent published peak-outflow regressions put this dam between 9,791 and 45,004 m³/s; the reported figure is **outside** that envelope.

### 7. Tiware Dam — Ratnagiri, Maharashtra, India, 2 July 2019

**What actually happened.** Neglected piping and wall fissures. Over 400 mm in a day exploited unaddressed seepages reported by villagers; the central bund collapsed at 21:30. The wave travelled about 18 km downstream (fast mountain torrent, depth not stated). Reported deaths: 23. Reported peak breach discharge **1,200 m³/s** (~ approximate in the source).

**What our system produced.** Case `piping` — The document names piping and pre-existing seepage paths. Peak outflow **1,169 m³/s** at 27.0 min, releasing 2.4 MCM of the 2.4 MCM stored. Breach parameters from: froehlich2008.

**Agreement.** 0.97× reported (-2.6%), graded *excellent*. Four independent published peak-outflow regressions put this dam between 1,167 and 3,074 m³/s; the reported figure is inside that envelope.

## Where it disagrees, and why

Two rows deserve to be read before the summary box is quoted anywhere.

### Malpasset is the one genuine outlier — and the reference figure is suspect

We compute 40,698 m³/s against a reported 8,000 m³/s — 5.09×, by far
the worst row in the table. Three things about that are worth stating rather
than burying:

1. The reference figure of 8,000 m³/s sits **below the entire envelope** of the four independent published
   peak-outflow regressions for a dam of this size (9,791–45,004 m³/s). Our value sits inside it.
2. The reference document is internally inconsistent here. It gives Malpasset
   ~8,000 m³/s and South Fork ~8,500 m³/s — near-identical peaks for a 66.5 m
   dam holding 50 MCM and a 22 m dam holding 18 MCM. Those two structures
   cannot plausibly have produced the same peak discharge.
3. A web search for a corroborating published peak breach discharge for
   Malpasset did not find one; the figures that surface are downstream
   modelled discharges at gauged sections, which are not comparable to a peak
   at the breach.

So this row is recorded as a disagreement, and it is **not** claimed as a
model error. It may be one. The evidence available says the reference number
is the more likely problem, and that is as far as the evidence goes.

### Banqiao depends on inputs we supplied

The reference gives Banqiao's storage and height but not its inflow or its
design spillway capacity, and the `spillway_blockage` case needs both. The
13,000 m³/s inflow and 1,742 m³/s design capacity used here are commonly
cited figures that we introduced. The 2.31× ratio is therefore partly a
statement about those two inputs, not only about the physics.

## What this exercise changed in the code

This validation was not a formality run at the end. It found two real bugs,
both of which are fixed and both of which moved these numbers:

1. **`foundation_failure` over-predicted St. Francis by 2.4×.** It drained
   the reservoir through a rectangle as wide as the crest and as deep as the
   dam, over a broad-crested weir. Both halves were wrong: a concrete dam
   stands in a gorge, so the crest length is the valley width at the *top*,
   and a full-height opening that appears in seconds is under critical-flow
   (Ritter) control, not weir control. Now 0.75×.

2. **`piping` produced a higher peak than `overtopping` on the same dam,**
   which is backwards — Froehlich's k₀ is 1.0 for piping against 1.3 for
   overtopping precisely because a piping breach is narrower. The orifice
   area grew to the full final breach cross-section while still under the
   entire reservoir head. It is now bounded by the weir discharge through
   the same opening. Teton went from 223,143 m³/s (3.43× reported) to
   102,736 m³/s (1.58×), and Tiware from 2,465 to 1,169 m³/s — 0.97× the
   reported 1,200.

Before those two fixes this table read 3/7 within a factor of 2 with a median
absolute error of 105.4%. It now reads 5/7 and 57.8%.

On Teton specifically: the reported 65,120 m³/s is the *right-abutment*
breach that actually occurred. The same literature assesses a centre
internal-erosion breach — which is what our model geometry represents — at
117,851 m³/s. Against that figure our 102,736 m³/s is 0.87×.
